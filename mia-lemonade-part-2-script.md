## CHAPTER 1 — Recap + Hook (0:00–1:15)

*[On screen: "PART 2: The Empire Strikes Back." Quick clips: lemonade cups, notebook, visors.]*

If you missed part one, my nine-year-old daughter Mia turned our front yard lemonade stand into a multi-location beverage franchise, hired three kids, merged with a competitor, and made two hundred and seventeen dollars in one weekend. And I said she was already planning her expansion into cookies.

Well. Parents. I need you to sit down, because you asked for part two, and part two is so much worse.

Because here's what I didn't tell you. When a nine-year-old makes more money than your part-time job, she does not celebrate. She schedules a meeting.

The Monday after that weekend, I came downstairs at 6:45 in the morning, and Mia was sitting at our kitchen table with a laminated agenda. Laminated. We own a laminator because of a school project from two years ago, and I had completely forgotten about it. She slid a copy across the table to me and said, "Mom, we have a lot to cover, and I only have until 7:30 before I need to brief my team."

Her team. At 6:45 in the morning. On a Monday. In June.

The agenda had four items. Cookies. Insurance. A logo. And, I swear, "Dad's coffee problem."

I took a sip of my own coffee, and I realized this was no longer a cute summer thing. This was a hostile takeover of my kitchen. And it was only getting started.

## CHAPTER 2 — The Cookie Division (1:15–4:15)

*[On screen: "Item #1: Cookies." Flour, cookie sheets, a hand-drawn menu.]*

Let's start with the cookies, because that's where the kitchen went from "child's hobby" to "commercial bakery."

Mia did not want to bake regular chocolate chip cookies. She said, and I quote, "Mom, chocolate chip is a commodity. Everybody has chocolate chip. I need a signature item."

A commodity. She's nine.

So for three days, our kitchen smelled like a cafe in Paris. She tested lavender honey shortbread, which, naturally, matched her best-selling lemonade. She tested brown butter sea salt cookies. She tested something called a "strawberry basil thumbprint," which I was told to refer to as a "limited release."

She made me and my husband taste-test every batch and fill out a rating card. Yes, a card. One to five stars, with a space for comments. My husband gave the thumbprint a four and wrote, "A little dry." Mia read it, looked at him with the calm of a Fortune 500 CEO, and said, "Dad, I'm going to need you to be more specific."

He said, "It's a cookie, Mia."

She said, "Then you're not my target demographic," and took his card away.

He has not recovered.

Here's where the math gets wild. She worked out the cost per cookie down to the penny. Butter, flour, sugar, honey, even the cost of the little paper bags. She figured out that if she sold two cookies for three dollars, she'd make about a 70 percent margin. I did not know what a margin was until I was thirty-one. Mia knew by nine and a half, and she says it like she's mad at me for not knowing.

Then she hit a problem. Mia can't use the oven alone. We have a rule. So did she give up? Of course not. She hired my mother-in-law.

Yes. She sat down with her grandmother, offered her what she called "a consulting fee" of one free cookie per batch, and my mother-in-law, who has been baking since before I was born, is now an employee of a fourth grader. She told me last week, "I don't know how it happened, but I'm happy." That's the thing about Mia. People walk away from a deal with her thinking it was their idea.

By Friday, Mia had forty-eight cookies ready, a printed menu, and a hand-drawn sign that said "Lavender Honey Shortbread: The Flavor That Made Your Neighbor Jealous."

Weird flex number one: she named her bakery before she'd sold a single cookie. It's called "Mia's Sweet Spot." She had business cards. They said "Founder and CEO." The font was Papyrus. We are working on that.

## CHAPTER 3 — The Health Inspector Scare (4:15–7:30)

*[On screen: "Item #2: Insurance. (Oh no.)" A knock on the door. A clipboard.]*

Saturday arrived and the stand was packed. Mrs. Chen's driveway was jammed with cars, and the line for lemonade and cookies stretched past the mailbox. By nine in the morning, Mia had already sold thirty-one cookies. And that's when a man in a gray polo shirt walked up with a clipboard.

My stomach dropped. I knew exactly who he was before he said a word. He had that look. You know the look. A man whose entire job is to find out what you did wrong.

He introduced himself. He worked for the county. And he said, very politely, "I'm here because we received a call about unlicensed food sales on this street."

Parents, I felt my soul leave my body. I'm standing there thinking: I am about to get my nine-year-old shut down by the county, and there will be a form, and I will be the person on the form.

I opened my mouth to apologize. And Mia stepped in front of me.

She stuck out her hand, looked him dead in the eye, and said, "Good morning, sir. I appreciate you coming out. I'd be happy to walk you through our food safety practices."

Then she did. For ten minutes.

She showed him the gloves she'd bought. She showed him the hand sanitizer station. She showed him the cooler with a thermometer in it, which, by the way, she had been checking every thirty minutes and logging in her notebook. She had a log. A temperature log. She pointed out that the cookies were individually wrapped, that no one touched food without gloves, and that every ingredient list was printed on a label in case of allergies.

I did not know about any of this. I genuinely did not know. She had watched a health department video on YouTube University, and she had implemented it without telling anyone.

The inspector flipped through her notebook. He read for a really long time. Then he looked up at me and said, "Ma'am, I have inspected restaurants that don't keep records this good."

And then he explained the actual issue. Technically, for homemade baked goods, she needed a cottage food permit, and the lemonade needed to follow some specific rules. He wasn't there to shut her down. He said the complaint had come from someone else, and he was just doing his job. Then he handed Mia a pamphlet, and he said, "If you fill this out, you'll be fully legal by next weekend."

Mia took the pamphlet. She said, "Thank you, sir. Would you like a free lemonade for the road?"

He took it. He took the lavender honey. And as he was leaving he said, "That's, uh, really good." And he bought two cookies.

Weird flex number two: a nine-year-old made a county health inspector buy her product. That's not a lemonade stand. That's lobbying.

But I want you to remember something he said: "The complaint came from someone else." Because I did not forget it. And neither did Mia.

## CHAPTER 4 — The Mystery Complaint (7:30–10:30)

*[On screen: "Who called the county?" Red string, sticky notes, a detective-style notebook.]*

That afternoon, Mia called an emergency meeting at our kitchen table. She wrote the words "WHO CALLED?" at the top of a fresh page in her notebook.

We narrowed it down fast. Because in our cul-de-sac, there's only one person with enough free time, enough motivation, and enough lawn signs to make an anonymous complaint. It was the Hendersons.

Now, let me explain. The Hendersons live at the end of the street, and their son Brandon, who is eleven, runs a lemonade stand every single summer. He's been running it for three years. And Brandon's stand, I'm sorry to say, is a card table with a pitcher of powdered lemonade and a sign that says "LEMONADE $1." Some kids have a gift, and Brandon's is not business.

But this year, Mia's numbers had completely demolished his. Brandon went from making about twelve dollars on a good Saturday to making about three. And his mother, Mrs. Henderson, had been giving Mia some very tight smiles at the mailbox.

So I assumed it was her. I told Mia, "Let's just let it go. Don't start a feud."

Mia looked at me like I had suggested we give away the company. She said, "Mom, I'm not starting a feud. I'm starting a conversation."

And here's where my daughter does something that I still think about. She did not send a angry note. She didn't egg anyone's house. She went back to her notebook, and she wrote down Brandon's prices, his hours, and his location. Then she went to the Hendersons' door, by herself, with a plate of cookies, and I watched from the window like a hostage negotiator's assistant.

Here's what I heard her say. "Hi, Mrs. Henderson. I'm Mia. I think there might have been a misunderstanding about the permits, and I wanted to make sure Brandon and I are on the same page. I have a proposal."

Mrs. Henderson, who had been planning, I'm now convinced, to say something very rude, just stood there. Because a nine-year-old with a plate of cookies and a proposal is very hard to be mad at.

Mia's proposal was this. Brandon would join the business as head of "product development," because, and she said this to his face, "Brandon, your idea for the frozen lemonade slushies is really good, and I haven't figured out how to do it." Brandon had one good idea, and Mia had found it, and now it was the future of the company.

His mom, to my complete shock, said yes.

The Hendersons did not call the county. It turned out it was a guy two streets over who just didn't like the foot traffic. But Mia never found that out, and honestly, she didn't need to. She walked into a rivalry and walked out with a new department.

Weird flex number three: she turned a suspect into a stakeholder. I can't even get my coworkers to return an email.

## CHAPTER 5 — The Employee Uprising (10:30–13:30)

*[On screen: "Item #3: Payroll." Tiny paychecks, a little handwritten petition.]*

Now. Let's talk about labor.

By week three, Mia's company had six employees. Tommy on the register. Beth on inventory. Noah running the satellite location. Brandon in product development. And her cousin Jake, who was now officially "Director of Logistics," which meant he rode his bike. Plus my mother-in-law, who, again, works for a child.

And every one of them was working for popsicles.

On a Wednesday, I'm folding laundry, and I hear a knock on the door. It's Beth. Ten years old, holding a piece of paper. And she says, very formally, "Mrs. Mia's mom, I need to speak to you."

I said, "I'm not Mia's HR department, sweetheart."

She said, "Then who is?"

I didn't have an answer.

It turns out Beth had written a petition. An actual petition, signed by Tommy, Noah, Brandon, and Jake. It listed three demands. One: more than popsicles. Two: a day off. Three, which was my favorite: "a bathroom policy."

I took the petition to Mia. I braced for a meltdown. Instead, she read the whole thing, nodded, and said, "These are fair concerns."

I said, "You're not mad?"

She said, "Mom, a unhappy workforce is a liability. I've been waiting for someone to say something."

She'd been waiting. For the uprising. She'd budgeted for it.

Within the hour, she called a staff meeting on our back patio. She'd made a handout. She announced that from now on, every employee would earn a commission, five percent of whatever they personally sold, paid out every Sunday in cash. They would get a mandatory day off on Wednesdays. And there would be a bathroom policy, which was "go whenever you want, just tell Tommy so he can cover."

The kids cheered. Beth actually cried a little. Tommy, who is eight, asked if he could get a business card too, and Mia said, "Not until you hit your quarterly goal."

Quarterly. He's eight. He saluted her.

And here's the thing about that Sunday payout. Beth made eleven dollars in commission. Eleven dollars! And she was so proud, she took a picture of the money and sent it to her grandmother. Her grandmother called me. She said, "Who is this girl, and can she run for office?"

Weird flex number four: she dealt with a labor strike in under an hour, and nobody got fired. Our actual government should take notes.

## CHAPTER 6 — The Big Order (13:30–16:30)

*[On screen: "The phone rings." Dramatic zoom on a ringing phone.]*

Okay. Chapter six. This is where it stopped being a story about a kid and started being a story about a lot of money.

It's a Thursday evening. My phone rings. It's a number I don't recognize. And the woman on the other end says, "Hi, is this the mother of Mia, the lemonade girl?"

I said, "Uh. Yes?"

She said, "My name is Patricia. I'm the events coordinator for the Riverside Community Festival. We have a food vendor that cancelled, and we were told your daughter might be able to help."

Told by who? Apparently, the health inspector. The guy in the gray polo. He had gone back to his office and told people about the kid with the temperature log.

Patricia said the festival was in ten days. Two thousand people expected. And she needed someone to provide fresh lemonade and baked goods for the whole weekend. She offered six hundred dollars for the booth, plus Mia could keep whatever she sold.

Six hundred. For the booth.

I handed the phone to Mia, who was doing her homework at the kitchen table, and I mouthed, "It's a festival."

Mia took the phone. She put on what I can only call her boardroom voice. And she said, "Hello, Patricia. Thank you for reaching out. Before I commit, I have a few questions. What's the booth size? Is there electrical access? Are there other beverage vendors? And what's your cancellation policy?"

I was standing in my own kitchen thinking, who is this woman?

Patricia answered every single question. Then Mia said, "I'll need to discuss it with my team, and I'll get back to you by tomorrow at noon."

She hung up. She turned to me and said, "Never accept on the first call, Mom. It shows you're desperate."

She had no idea she was quoting a negotiation book. She'd found it at the library. Fourth grade, ages 9 to 12 section. She'd read it twice.

The next ten days were basically a military operation. Mia filled out the cottage food permit and my husband drove her to the county office, where the clerk actually recognized her name. She ordered two hundred cups with her logo. She recruited eight more kids, trained them in two sessions, and made a schedule that rotated shifts so nobody worked more than two hours straight. She even assigned a "wellness captain," whose job was to make sure everyone drank water.

And by the way, my husband put in a good word and got the portable card reader upgraded, so this time, there was no Apple Pay incident.

The night before the festival, I peeked into her bedroom, and she was asleep with the notebook open on her chest. On the last page, in her handwriting, it said: "Goal: $1,000. Stretch goal: $1,500. Remember to smile."

Remember to smile. I stood in that doorway for a long time.

## CHAPTER 7 — Festival Day (16:30–18:15)

*[On screen: Crowd, banners, a long line. "Saturday, 8:00 AM."]*

Festival morning. We arrived at 6:30 to set up, and Mia had already organized the booth layout on paper. Cookies on the left, lemonade on the right, samples in the middle, and the card reader at the exit so nobody could walk away without paying. I'm not making that up. She called it "friction-free checkout."

By ten o'clock, the line was forty people deep.

By noon, we had sold out of lavender honey shortbread. And Mia was out in the parking lot, with Jake on his bike, running a supply route like a tiny general.

I watched a grown man in a suit, who was clearly there representing some company, wait in line for fifteen minutes. And when he got to the front, he said, "I heard about this booth from three different people. Who's in charge here?"

Mia said, "I am, sir. Would you like to try the strawberry basil?"

He said, "How old are you?"

She said, "Nine and three-quarters."

He laughed. Then he bought forty dollars' worth of product and left a card. I looked at it later. He ran a catering company. He said that if Mia ever wanted a summer internship, she should call him.

She was nine and three-quarters. And she's now got a standing job offer.

At the end of the weekend, we counted everything at our kitchen table. The cash, the card reader, the commission payouts, the supply costs. Mia did the math in her notebook, and she put down her pencil, and she was very quiet.

Then she said, "Mom. I made one thousand, one hundred and forty-two dollars."

She had missed her stretch goal by about three hundred and sixty dollars. And for a second, I thought she was going to be upset.

## CHAPTER 8 — The Ending (18:15–19:15)

*[On screen: Soft music. The kitchen table. A single dollar bill taped into a notebook.]*

Instead, she looked at the money, and then she looked up at me, and she said, "I want to take out two hundred dollars, and I want to give it to the food bank."

I said, "Mia, that's a lot. That's a lot of your money."

She said, "Mom, I can make more. That's the whole point."

Then she divided the rest into three envelopes. One for her college fund. One for reinvestment. And one marked "Dad's coffee," which she slid across the table, because, and I quote, "he's been very supportive, and he needs to be able to afford a better cookie review."

My husband has not stopped laughing.

So here's my answer to the question I asked you in part one. What's the best weird flex but okay moment you've seen from your child? For me, it's not the card reader. It's not the staff meetings, or the festival, or even the health inspector.

It's that a nine-year-old turned every single obstacle, a complaint, a rival, a strike, a stranger on the phone, into a partnership. And then she gave some of it away.

She's already told me what's next. She wants to start a "Junior Entrepreneur Club" at her school. She's drafted the bylaws.

And I'll tell you about it in part three.
